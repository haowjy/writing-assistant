## What was added and why

Genres are story kinds; styles are prose registers; tropes are familiar story shapes; situations are concrete predicaments; and continuity challenges are consistency problems a task asks the writer to manage. Composition rules govern how those ingredients are used. The table shows that all requested additions were supplied, including six composition rules within the requested range of four to six.

| Catalog list | Existing entries | Added | Merged | Requested addition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Genres | 60 | 20 | 80 | 20 |
| Styles | 20 | 16 | 36 | 16 |
| Tropes | 40 | 40 | 80 | 40 |
| Situations | 32 | 48 | 80 | 48 |
| Continuity challenges | 12 | 18 | 30 | 18 |
| Composition rules | 7 | 6 | 13 | 4–6 |
| Candidate books (`book_candidates`) | 15 | 0 | 15 | Unchanged |

Of the situations, 24 emphasise unresolved choices that should prompt a question or a stated default. The other 24 emphasise concrete scenes in new physical and social settings: markets, factories, construction sites, mines, warehouses, hospitals, courtrooms, ships, farms, kitchens, laboratories, software workplaces, stadiums, festivals, classrooms and wildfires, among others.

The continuity additions require the writer to arrange events: reveal or withhold information at the right time, let a character learn, pay off an earlier detail, or leave a detail inactive until later. The six composition rules address unstated defaults, checking length, varying wording and settings across a batch, making tropes affect decisions, specifying the timing and consequence of continuity events, and making every genre in a blend affect the scene.

The brief's “weighted toward PROSE-WRITING situations” was interpreted as concrete scenes suitable for prose in the requested settings. All situations imply something that can be dramatised, and most contain an explicit open decision. This was an interpretation, not a measured effect on model output.
