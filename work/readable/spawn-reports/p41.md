# Identify recurring weaknesses in the generated prose

Background agent p41 was asked to explain why the generated writing read poorly, using actual passages and counted habits. It completed a prose analysis with a count and a verbatim quotation for every named habit; it also rejected the proposed explanation that conflicts were routinely resolved too quickly.

The `custom50` suite contains 50 writing tasks. An “attempt” is one run of a task. The two comparison groups, called arms in the source, use different model-weight formats: `nf4` is a 4-bit quantized format, and `bf16` is a 16-bit brain-floating-point format. Quantization stores weights at reduced precision. Explicit briefs state more requirements; loose briefs leave more creative choices open.

Task identifiers combine a family and a case number, as in F2-02. F1 asks for a reply scene; F2 asks for local revision or exploration followed by writing; F3 asks for alternatives; F4 asks for construction or updating of a knowledge base (KB), the project reference files; and F5 asks for writing using retrieved project facts.

## Repeated prose habits and their consequences

The main sample was 25 distinct graded prose pieces from F1, F2 and F5. Unless stated otherwise, the fractions below count pieces in that sample. The H identifiers are this report's numbered habits, retained so the reader can find the supporting passages. The prose-quality rubric is Q2; its cited scores are out of 5, with lower scores indicating weaker writing.

1. H1, atmospheric openings, occurred in 21/25 pieces without being requested. Repeated use of atmosphere as the opening event also appeared in the bf16 repeated-prompt sample: 12/25 began “The damp air of the archive”.
2. H2, explanatory phrases attached after a noun or clause, appeared in 25/25 documents, with 122 hits. Possessive appositives appeared in 23/25 and em-dash appositives in 20/25. An appositive renames or explains a noun; participial afterthoughts add descriptions of actions or states. The report attributes the language score of 2.20 to this accumulating description.
3. H3, stating inner feelings or knowledge instead of dramatising them, included `felt` in 21/25 pieces, with 47 hits; `seemed` in 13/25; and `knew/knowledge` in 12/25. The report links this habit to the characterization score of 2.24.
4. H4, phrases the report describes as memorised, included “felt the familiar knot tighten” in 4/25 pieces and “familiar knot” in 6/25. The count demonstrates reuse; it does not independently establish how the phrases were learned.
5. H5, restating a mood, included 16.8 abstract nouns per 1,000 words and either `thick` or `heavy` in 22/25 pieces. The report calls this a direct cause of the redundancy score of 2.00: repeated statements of the same emotional condition add words without changing the scene. That causal attribution is the analyst's judgement.
6. H6, ending on a named abstraction, occurred in 22/25 pieces.
7. H7, paired rhetorical clauses, included “not just X; it was Y” in 10/25 pieces and `, but` in 20/25.
8. H8, summarising repeated activity instead of staging a scene, appeared through iterative markers in 22/25 pieces. Dialogue accounted for only 23% of sentences overall; even briefs asking for dialogue-led work produced 24–37%. The report connects this lack of immediate action to the pacing score of 2.28.
9. H9, over-explaining motive, is illustrated by F1-06, F1-08 and F5-10.
10. H10, copying the brief or notes into the work and slipping into screenplay presentation, included verbatim brief reuse in 4/25 pieces, meta stage directions in 3/25 and script format in 4/25. All occurred under loose briefs; explicit briefs suppressed them.
11. H11, exceeding the requested length, affected 9/10 explicit pieces with a 120–220-word budget. Their median length was 268 words.

More explicit instructions changed compliance with content and format, including verbatim copying, but the report found no improvement in craft quality. Atmospheric openings, mood restatement, stated feelings and appositives occurred at equal or higher rates under loose briefs. Loose pieces had a median of 428 words, compared with 268 under explicit briefs.

The evidence did not support “conflict resolved too fast”: 22/25 endings suspended or escalated conflict, and F5-07 was the sole early resolution. Hedge and adverb accumulation existed, with 17.9 words ending in `-ly` per 1,000 words, but was secondary. The sample was too small for claims about individual genres.

## What the agent read and produced

That made 100 prose pieces available, with about 35 read closely. A broader 150-attempt corpus included outputs, briefs, workspace files and metadata. Reproducible regular-expression and word-sequence counts were run over the sample.

The final `craft.md` contained 23,091 bytes and 434 lines and was verified to exist and be non-empty at `/home/jimyao/.meridian/projects/orange-juniper-leaf/spawns/p34/work/transcript-analysis/craft.md`. Read the [repository copy](../../transcript-analysis/craft.md) for counted quotations. F3 produces directions and F4 produces a continuity wiki, so neither supports this prose-quality analysis. The report therefore qualifies the requested “all five families” coverage: its prose findings apply to F1, F2 and F5, with the same habits observed in all three.
